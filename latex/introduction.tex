\section{Introduction}
In the interior of the most massive of stars, three $\alpha$ particles are constantly being fused into carbon-12. The energy released by this reaction counteracts the endless pressure from the colossal weight of the envelope, creating equilibrium, at least for a time. Due to changes in its composition, gravity eventually wins this tug of war, and the star meets a violent end, scattering its carbon into deep space. This process has been repeated so many times that carbon has become the fourth most abundant element by mass, with \ce{^12C} its most common form. With so much carbon available, it has become a fundamental building block of all known life; so essential that we can hardly imagine existence without it. \\

Since some of the first research of the carbon isotopes were carried out at the Cavendish Laboratory \note{ref}, it has been the subject of countless other studies. From the prediction and following discovery of the Hoyle state \note{ref} which is so essential for stellar nucleosynthesis, to current medicinal studies of its uses in proton therapy \note{ref} - its relevance cannot be understated. We will in this thesis work primarily with the \SI{17.76}{MeV} $0^+$ resonance along with its immediate neighbours, the \SI{16.62}{MeV} $2^-$ and \SI{18.35}{MeV} $3^-$ resonances. Due to its accessibility, much work has already been done on the lower-lying $2^-$ resonance, primarily in determining branching ratios for its decay to \ce{^8Be}. Only recently has the $0^+$ state been opened up to deeper investigation, with this thesis being the first to examine it in great detail. \note{ensure this is right. expand on why it was more accessible. }

\subsection{Brief review of the \SI{17.76}{MeV} resonance}
Since the binding energy of a proton in \ce{^12C} is \SI{15.96}{MeV}, levels above this threshold can easily be reached by bombarding \ce{^11B} with protons. Specific states, such as the \SI{17.76}{MeV} resonance, can then be formed by tuning the proton beam energy. Since these resonances are all located well above the triple-$\alpha$ threshold of \SI{7.27}{MeV}, in a sequential picture one decay path will proceed through an intermediate \ce{^8Be} resonance. Based on the energetics of this reaction, the intermediate resonance can either be the ground state \ce{^11B(p, \alpha_0)^8Be} ($\alpha_0$ channel) or the first excited state \ce{^11B(p, \alpha_1)^8Be^*} ($\alpha_1$ channel). The basic idea is then to use properties of the measured $3\alpha$ final state, such as its momentum distribution, to deduce properties of the initial \ce{^12C} resonance. The \SI{17.76}{MeV} has not received much interest throughout the years, and what studies did include it has mainly been broad surveys of the higher nuclear levels of \ce{^12C}. One of the earliest studies of this type was performed by Segel et al. \cite{Segel} back in 1965, where they noted that due to emission through the $\alpha_0$ channel, the resonance must have natural parity ($\pi = (-1)^J$). Based on a Legendre series fit to their data, they could also tentatively label it as a $0^+$ resonance, although they also saw a substantial $a_3$ component, indicating interference from possibly the nearby \SI{18.35}{MeV} $3^-$ resonance. Later studies \cite{Hanna}\cite{Munch} supports this classification. \note{ask Hans about importance of e.g. Giorni, Bronson}. \\
