\section{Introduction}
In the interior of the most massive of stars, three $\alpha$-particles are constantly being fused into carbon-12. The energy released by this reaction counteracts the endless pressure from the colossal weight of the envelope, creating equilibrium, at least for a time. Due to changes in its composition, gravity eventually wins this tug of war, and the star meets a violent end, scattering its carbon into deep space. This process has been repeated so many times that carbon has become the fourth most abundant element by mass, with \ce{^12C} its most common form. With so much carbon available, it has become a fundamental building block of all known life; so essential that we can hardly imagine existence without it. \\

Since some of the first research on \ce{^12C} was carried out at the Cavendish Laboratory \cite{Oliphant}\cite{Gilbert} almost 85 years ago, it has been the subject of countless other studies. From the prediction and following discovery of the Hoyle state \cite{Hoyle}\cite{Dunbar} which is so essential for stellar nucleosynthesis, to current medicinal studies of its uses in proton therapy \cite{Cirrone} - its relevance cannot be understated. We will in this thesis work primarily with the \SI{17.76}{MeV} $0^+$ resonance along with its immediate neighbours, the \SI{16.62}{MeV} $2^-$ and \SI{18.35}{MeV} $3^-$ resonances. Due to its lower energy and larger cross section, most of the early studies were on the lower-lying $2^-$ resonance, and primarily in determining its branching ratio to \ce{^8Be}. It was only later that the $0^+$ resonance started receiving attention, and that mostly in the form of larger surveys of the continuum of states in the area. Thus this thesis is one of the first to examine it in greater detail.

\subsection{Brief review of the resonances}
Since the binding energy of a proton in \ce{^12C} is \SI{15.96}{MeV}, levels above this threshold can easily be reached by bombarding \ce{^11B} with protons. Specific states, such as the \SI{17.76}{MeV} resonance, can then be formed by tuning the proton beam energy. Since these resonances are all located well above the triple-$\alpha$ threshold of \SI{7.27}{MeV}, in a sequential picture one decay path will proceed through an intermediate \ce{^8Be} resonance. Based on the energetics of this reaction, the intermediate resonance can either be the ground state \ce{^11B(p, \alpha_0)^8Be} ($\alpha_0$ channel) or the first excited state \ce{^11B(p, \alpha_1)^8Be^*} ($\alpha_1$ channel). The basic idea is then to use properties of the measured $3\alpha$ final state, such as its momentum distribution, to deduce properties of the initial \ce{^12C} resonance. See figure \ref{fig: introduction: energy levels} for an overview of the energies involved. \\

The \SI{16.62}{MeV} resonance is the easiest of the three to reach, since it only requires beam energies of around \SI{670}{keV}. Combined with its larger cross-section, it was an obvious target for the early studies. Due to its unnatural parity, transitions through the $\alpha_0$ channel are not allowed. Thus its decays can proceed only through the $\alpha_1$ channel, in which both angular momentum $l=1$ and $l=3$ are allowed. One would then expect the $l=1$ component to dominate due to the angular momentum barrier. However, this is not what Quebert and Marquez \cite{Quebert} found in 1969: they found that their data could only be described if $l=3$ was the primary decay. Almost twenty years later, Becker, Rolfs and Trautvetter \cite{Becker} reached the opposite conclusion. A recent study by Stave et al. \cite{Stave} then reached the same conclusion as Quebert and Marquez. This back-and-forth has been discussed extensively by Kuhlwein \cite{Kuhlwein}, who found that the contradictory results are due to differences in the nuclear models and using one-dimensional projections of the two-dimensional phase-space distribution. By allowing both components into a single fit to the full distribution, they found a ratio of roughly $(l = 1)/(l = 3) \sim 0.8$ to be the best match. \\

The \SI{17.76}{MeV} resonance has not received quite as much attention throughout the years, and what studies did include it has mainly been broad surveys of the continuum of nuclear levels in the area. One of the earliest studies of this type was performed by Symons and Treacy back in 1963 \cite{Symons}, who could, based on a Legendre series fit to its angular distribution, label it as a $0^+$ state. Two years later, a study by Segel et al. \cite{Segel} supported this assignment, but also noted that they saw a substantial $a_3$ component, indicating interference from possibly the nearby \SI{18.35}{MeV} $3^-$ resonance. Both studies found the state to be resonant on the $\alpha_0$ band, indicating that it must have natural parity. Later studies \cite{Hanna}\cite{Munch} supports these observations. \\

The \SI{18.35}{MeV} resonance shares a similar history with the \SI{17.76}{MeV} due to their high \SI{>2}{MeV} beam energy requirement. One of its first mentions was in a study of the angular distribution of gamma rays by Gove and Paul in 1955 \cite{Gove}, who tentatively labeled it as a $2^+$ state, but noted that this could possibly be due to interference from other nearby states. A few years later, a study by Symons and Treacy \cite{Symons} reached the same conclusion, although they decisively labeled it as a $2^+$ state. It was only during the study by Segel et al. \cite{Segel} that it received its current classification as a $3^-$ state. Later research based on its $\gamma$ decays \cite{Hanna} supports this new classification.
