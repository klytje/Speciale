\section{Theory} \label{section: theory}
Since we are dealing almost exclusively with many-body systems, one of the primary goals of nuclear physicists is to construct simplified models of nuclear systems. Although many such phenomenological models have been discovered through the years, a single overarching framework which can consistently model all phenomena has yet to be found. In this thesis we are mainly interested in models of nuclear interactions, which is well described by the two branches of R-matrix theory. Thus most of our effort in this chapter will go into understanding that theory.

\subsection{The compound nucleus}
One of the early theories of nuclear reactions is the idea of a compound nucleus as proposed by Bohr in 1936 \cite{Bohr}. The basic idea is that the incident nucleus is momentarily captured by the target, thus creating an intermediate compound state. The energy of the incident nucleus is then redistributed throughout this compound state due to internal interactions. Central to this description is the idea that the formation and decay of the compound nucleus occurs \textit{independently} of each other. \\

Further work on the model proved to be very successful, yet also unsatisfactory: it depends on the Weisskopf-Wigner theory \note{citation} of resonance absorption and subsequent emission of radiation, and through that, on time-dependent perturbation theory. This is problematic as the nuclear forces can certainly not be treated as mere perturbations. The solution to this problem eventually led to the development of the R-matrix theory, which is the topic of the next section. 

\subsection{R-matrix theory}
This whole section on R-matrix theory follows the general steps used in the lecture notes by D. Baye \cite{Baye lecture notes}. \\ 

Consider the potential scattering of some arbitrary partial wave. A core assumption of R-matrix theory is that the potential $V$ only differs from the Coulomb potential $V_C$ by a short-range term, such that
\begin{align*}
	V(r) \xrightarrow[r \rightarrow \infty]{} V_C(r) = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}
\end{align*}

The central idea of the theory is that beyond some specific radius $r = a$, the short-range term can be neglected. Thus this interaction radius $a$ effectively divides space into two regions: an internal region, where the nuclear interactions must be accounted for, and an external region, where only the Coulomb interaction matters. The internal region is treated as a confined system, allowing the total wave function to be expanded into a finite set of eigenstates. In the external region, the wave function can be approximated by its asymptotic value, leaving only an unknown phase shift. By matching the two wave functions at the boundary, this phase shift can be identified. 

\subsubsection{The internal and external wave functions}
Assume that we have two particles with the reduced mass $\mu$, which interacts through the central potential $V$ with some positive energy 
\begin{align*}
	E = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2\mu}
\end{align*}

By applying the Schrödinger equation to this situation, we get
\begin{align*}
	\bigg(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu} \Delta + V(r)\bigg)\psi(\bm{r}) = E\psi(\bm{r})
\end{align*}

Since we are dealing with a central potential, the wave function can be factorized into its radial and spherical components
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: factorization}
	\psi(r, \theta, \phi) = R_l(r) Y^m_l(\theta, \phi)
\end{align}

Note that in contrast to the usual factorization, $R_l$ also depends on the orbital angular momentum $l$ since we are still dealing with only a single partial wave. To simplify matters a bit, we make the change of variables $u(r) = rR(r)$, such that (\ref{theory: r-matrix: factorization}) becomes
\begin{align*}
	\psi(r, \theta, \phi) = \frac{u_l(r) Y^m_l(\theta, \phi)}{r}
\end{align*}

This allows us to write the radial part of the Schrödinger equation as
\begin{align*}
	\bigg[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu} \bigg(\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2} \bigg) + V(r)\bigg]u_l(r) = Eu_l(r)	
\end{align*}

Defining everything on the left-hand side as the Hamiltonian $H_l$, the equation can be restated more simply as
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: schrodinger}
	(H_l - E)u_l = 0
\end{align}

We are interested in the bounded radial solutions $u_l$ of (\ref{theory: r-matrix: schrodinger}) vanishing at the origin $u_l(0) = 0$ \note{why}. The positive energy solutions to this have the asymptotic behaviour 
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: solution 1}
	u_l(r) \xrightarrow[r\rightarrow \infty]{} F_l(\eta, \rho) \cos \delta_l  + G_l(\eta, \rho) \sin \delta_l 
\end{align}
where $F_l$ and $G_l$ are the regular and irregular Coulomb functions, and $\delta_l$ the phase shift. The arguments to the Coulomb functions are $\rho = kr$ and the Sommerfeld parameter $\eta = Z_1 Z_2 e^2 / 4 \pi \epsilon_0 \hbar v$, with $v = \sqrt{2E/\mu}$ the relative velocity of the interacting nuclei. It can be shown that the two Coulomb functions satisfies the Wronskian (temporarily suppressing $\rho$ and $\eta$) $F_l' G_l - G_l' F_l = 1$, which will be useful later. \\

Another possible solution is given as
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: solution 2}
u_l(r) \xrightarrow[r\rightarrow \infty]{} I_l(\eta, \rho)  - U_l O_l(\eta, \rho) 
\end{align}
where $I_l = G_l - iF_l$ and $O_l = G_l + iF_l$ are the incoming and outgoing Coulomb functions, and $U_l$ the scattering matrix defined by 
\begin{align*}
	U_l = e^{2i\delta_l}
\end{align*}

As per the base assumption of R-matrix theory, we can approximate the radial wave function $u^{\text{ext}}_l$ in the external region with the two solutions (\ref{theory: r-matrix: solution 1}) and (\ref{theory: r-matrix: solution 2}). The other base assumption allows us to expand the radial wave function $u^{\text{int}}_l$ for the internal region over some finite basis of linearly independent functions $\phi_j$:
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: internal expansion}
	u^{\text{int}}_l(r) = \sum^{N}_{j} c_j \varphi_j(r)
\end{align}

To ensure that the total wave function $u_l$ vanishes at the origin, so too must all $\phi_j$. Although they are not strictly required to be orthogonal at this stage, we will assume they are for later use. Neither are they required to satisfy any specific boundary condition at $r = a$. Instead, continuity of the total wave function $u_l$ and its first derivative across the boundary will be used to connect the internal and external wave functions. 

\subsubsection{Bloch operator}
At this stage, a major issue becomes apparent: the Hamiltonian $H_l$ is not Hermitian in the internal region:
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: hamiltonian hermitian}
\int_0^a fH_lgdr - \int_0^a gH_lfdr &= \bigg(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\bigg)\bigg[\int_0^a f \frac{d^2}{dr^2}gdr - \int_0^a g \frac{d^2}{dr^2}fdr \bigg] \nonumber \\ 
&= -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\bigg(f(a)g'(a) - g(a)f'(a)\bigg)
\end{align}

Bloch \cite{Bloch} neatly solved this issue by introducing a new surface operator
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{L} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\delta(r-a)\frac{d}{dr}
\end{align*}

The operator is specifically designed to cancel (\ref{theory: r-matrix: hamiltonian hermitian}), as can be seen by repeating the calculation:
\begin{align*}
	\int_0^a f \mathcal{L} g dr - \int_0^a g \mathcal{L} f dr &= \bigg(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\bigg)\bigg[\int_0^a f \delta(r\shortminus a)\frac{d}{dr}gdr - \int_0^a g \delta(r\shortminus a)\frac{d}{dr}fdr \bigg] \nonumber \\ 
	&= \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\bigg(f(a)g'(a) - g(a)f'(a)\bigg)
\end{align*}

Thus when combined with the Hamiltonian, $H_l + \mathcal{L}$, the total operator \textit{is} Hermitian. This new combined operator allows us to approximate the Schrödinger equation for the internal region by the Bloch-Schrödinger equation
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: Bloch-Schrödinger}
	(H_l + \mathcal{L} - E)u^{\text{int}}_l = \mathcal{L}u^{\text{int}}_l
\end{align}

This equation turns out to be equivalent to (see e.g. \cite{Baye lecture notes})
\begin{align*}
	(H_l - E)u^{\text{int}}_l = 0 && u_l^{\text{int} \prime}(a) = u_l^{\text{ext} \prime}(a)
\end{align*}
that is, the original Schrödinger equation (\ref{theory: r-matrix: schrodinger}) confined to the internal region, along with the first half of our continuity condition for the total wave function. Thus by supplementing it with the other half
\begin{align*}
	u^{\text{int}}_l(a) = u^{\text{ext}}_l(a)
\end{align*}
the Bloch operator guarantees continuity of the total wave function and its first derivative across the boundary. 

\subsubsection{The calculable R-matrix}
The namesake of the theory is defined by the equation
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: r-matrix}
	u_l(a) = R_l(a, E)au'_l(a)
\end{align}
Thus its inverse 
\begin{align*}
	R_l^{-1}(a, E) = a\frac{u'_l(a)}{u_l(a)}
\end{align*}
is the logarithmic derivative of the radial wave function evaluated at the boundary. So far, we have completely avoided one of the core concepts of R-matrix theory: channels. These channels corresponds to different projectile-target combinations, channel spins, and relative angular momenta. The R-matrix in the above equation is just a number; it relates one input channel to one output channel. In general, it will be a matrix containing an entry relating each pair of input and output channels. To obtain another representation of the matrix, the basis expansion of the internal wave function (\ref{theory: r-matrix: internal expansion}) is applied to the Bloch-Schrödinger equation (\ref{theory: r-matrix: Bloch-Schrödinger}), and subsequently projected onto the basis $\varphi_i(r)$:
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: r-matrix C}
	\sum_{j}^{N}\langle \varphi_i | T_l + \mathcal{L} + V - E | \varphi_j \rangle c_j = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu} \varphi_i(a)u^{\text{ext}\prime}_l(a)
\end{align}

Here it is convenient to introduce the symmetric $\bm{C}$-matrix
\begin{align*}
	\bm{C}_{ij}(E) = \langle \varphi_i | T_l + \mathcal{L} + V - E | \varphi_j \rangle
\end{align*}

By solving equation (\ref{theory: r-matrix: r-matrix C}) for the expansion coefficients $c_j$, we get the equation
\begin{align*}
	c_j = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u_l^{\text{ext}\prime}(a)\sum_{i}^{N}(\bm{C}^{-1})_{ij}\varphi_i(a)
\end{align*}

We can insert them back into the expansion equation (\ref{theory: r-matrix: internal expansion}) and compare with the first equation for the R-matrix (\ref{theory: r-matrix: r-matrix}) to get an equation for the calculable $R$-matrix
\begin{align*}
	R_l(a, E) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a}\sum_{i, j}^{N}\varphi_i(a)(\bm{C}^{-1})_{ij}\varphi_j(a)
\end{align*}

To complete this definition of $R$, we thus need to invert the $C$-matrix. To do this, we will finally need the orthonormality of the basis functions $\varphi_i(r)$. Remember that $\bm{C}$ implicitly depends on the energy $E$. Consider its non-zero eigenvalues $E_{nl}$ and corresponding eigenvectors $\bm{v}_{nl}$ evaluated for $E = 0$
\begin{align*}
	\bm{C}(0)\bm{v}_{nl} = E_{nl}\bm{v}_{nl}
\end{align*}

with the orthonormality condition $\bm{v}_{nl}^T \bm{v}_{nl} = \delta_{nn'}$. The requirements listed here are exactly what we need to invert it through its eigendecomposition, where the inverse is given as
\begin{align*}
	\bm{C}(0)^{-1} = \sum_n^N \frac{\bm{v}_{nl}\bm{v}_{nl}^T}{E_{nl}}
\end{align*}

This can easily be modified to work for any non-zero energy $E$: simply subtract it from the denominator, such that
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: C-inverse}
	\bm{C}(E)^{-1} = \sum_n^N \frac{\bm{v}_{nl}\bm{v}_{nl}^T}{E_{nl} - E}
\end{align}

Before we get to one of the primary results of R-matrix theory, there is a minor detail that should be corrected. So far we have worked with a finite basis, and thus everything is only approximately correct. To fix this issue, we can simply let $N$ tend toward infinity in all our sums. Going on with the derivation, we can now insert (\ref{theory: r-matrix: C-inverse}) into the definition of the $R$-matrix (\ref{theory: r-matrix: r-matrix C}) to get the equation
\begin{align*}
	R_l(E) &= \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a} \sum_{i, j}^{\infty} \phi_i(a)\bigg(\sum_n^\infty \frac{\bm{v}_{nl}\bm{v}_{nl}^T}{E_{nl} - E}\bigg)_{ij}\phi_j(a) \\
	&= \sum_{n}^{\infty} \frac{\gamma^2_{nl}}{E_{nl} - E}
\end{align*}

where $\gamma_{nl}$ is defined as 
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: reduced width}
	\gamma_{nl} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a}} \sum_{i}^{\infty}\bm{v}_{nl, i}\varphi_i(a)
\end{align}

$\gamma_{nl}$ is known as the reduced width amplitude. It received this name since its square is a crucial component of the formal width, as we will see in a moment. \note{ask Hans for proper terminology}\\

In general, the R-matrix has an infinity of real simple poles. If we instead approximate the solution, such that we only deal with a small number of poles, we will eventually arrive at the \textit{phenomenological} R-matrix. More specifically, if we approximate the matrix by a single pole with energy $E_1$ and reduced width $\gamma_{1,\ l}^2$, one can derive the equation (see e.g. \cite{Baye})
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: Gamma gamma}
	\Gamma = \frac{2 \gamma_{1l}^2 P(E_R)}{1 + \gamma_{1l}^2 S'(E_R)}
\end{align}
which relates the formal width $\Gamma$ to the reduced width $\gamma_{1\ l}^2$. Here $P(E_R)$ and $S'(E_R)$ are the penetrability and shift function, which will both be introduced in the next section. This equation will be relevant later when we want to compare simulations from different reaction models. 

\subsubsection{Applications} \label{sec: theory: r-matrix: applications}
One of our first applications of the theory is to determine the phase shift $\delta_l$ and the collision matrix $U_l$ from (\ref{theory: r-matrix: solution 1}) and (\ref{theory: r-matrix: solution 2}), respectively. Since we have already derived all of the necessary tools, this is simply a matter of substituting the two equations into the definition of the $R$-matrix (\ref{theory: r-matrix: r-matrix}). Throughout this section, all function dependencies will be suppressed for clarity, so remember that all of the Coulomb functions are evaluated at the boundary $\rho = ak$. \\

For the phase shift, we obtain the equation
\begin{align*}
	F_l \cos\delta_l  + G_l \sin\delta_l = \rho R_l\big[F_l'\cos\delta_l + G_l'\sin\delta_l \big]
\end{align*}

By solving for the phase shift $\delta_l$, we get the equation
\begin{align*}
	\tan\delta_l = -\frac{F_l - \rho R_l F_l'}{G_l - \rho R_l G_l'}
\end{align*}

We can repeat the exact same calculations for the scattering matrix to get the equation
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: scattering matrix}
	U_l = \frac{I_l - \rho R_l I_l'}{O_l - \rho R_l O_l'} = e^{2i\phi_l}\frac{1 - L_l^* R_l}{1 - L_l R_l}
\end{align}

The third term is obtained through some tedious calculations by defining the logarithmic derivative of $O_l$ at the channel radius, along with the hard-sphere phase shift $\phi_l$ as
\begin{align*}
	L_l = \rho \frac{O_l'}{O_l} && \phi_l = -\arctan \frac{F_l}{G_l}
\end{align*}

The meaning of these two equations are not that relevant for this discussion. It is worth noting that $L_l$ is typically an imaginary number, and so it is often split into its real and imaginary parts as
\begin{align*}
	L_l = S_l + iP_l
\end{align*}

where $S_l$ and $P_l$ are called the shift and penetrability factors. Through the definition of $L_l$ and the Wronskian relation satisfied by the Coulomb functions $F_l$ and $G_l$, a short derivation (see appendix \ref{appendix: shift and pen}) gives the equations for $P_l$ and $S_l$ as
\begin{align} \label{theory: r-matrix: penetration shift}
	P_l = \frac{\rho}{F_l^2 + G_l^2} && S_l = \rho\frac{F_l F_l' + G_l G_l'}{F_l^2 + G_l^2}	
\end{align}

These two equations are useful when we want to relate the reduced width $\gamma^2$ to the formal width $\Gamma$ through equation (\ref{theory: r-matrix: Gamma gamma}). 

\subsubsection{Three-body decays} \label{sec: theory: r-matrix: three body}
So far, the R-matrix theory can only describe two-body decays. To extend the model to also cover three-body decays, we assume that the decay occurs sequentially, i.e. \ce{^12C \rightarrow ^8Be + \alpha \rightarrow $3$\alpha}. The idea is then that we can use the standard R-matrix model to describe the first decay of a definite \ce{^12C} state to all possible intermediate \ce{^8Be} states. We can then again use the standard model to describe each decay from the states of \ce{^8Be} to the definite final state \ce{$3$\alpha}. \\

By using these extensions, one can derive similar equations as for the two-body model, which will not be discussed here. This sequential three-body model is used internally in some of the simulation tools which will be introduced later. 

\subsection{Kinematics}
We will later have need of some basic kinematic results for a sequential decay, which will be introduced in this section. A useful way of visualizing three-body decays will also be introduced. 

\subsubsection{Basic results}
If we define all our energies with respect to the ground state of \ce{^12C}, we can write the energy released in the first and second decay step as $Q_1 = E_{\ce{^12C}} - E_{\ce{^8Be}}$ and $Q_2 = E_{\ce{^8Be}} - E_{3\alpha}$, respectively. The energies of the individual $\alpha$-particles can then be calculated through conservation laws to be
\begin{align}
	&E_1 = \frac{2}{3}Q_1 \label{theory: kinematics: E1}\\
	&E_2 = \frac{1}{6}Q_1 + \frac{1}{2}Q_2 + \cos \alpha \sqrt{\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{3}} \label{theory: kinematics: E2}\\
	&E_3 = \frac{1}{6}Q_1 + \frac{1}{2}Q_2 - \cos \alpha \sqrt{\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{3}} \label{theory: kinematics: E3}
\end{align}
where $\alpha$ is the angle between the axis defined by the first decay and the second $\alpha$-particle, see figure \ref{fig: theory: kinematics: angles}. As this angle will be important later, it is worthwhile to write down its defining equation
\begin{align} \label{theory: kinematics: theta}
	2\cos \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{3}{Q_1 Q_2}}(E_2 - E_3)
\end{align}

\subsubsection{The Dalitz plot}
The goal of this thesis is to understand the excited states of \ce{^12C} based on the three $\alpha$-particles resulting from its decay. We can describe each of these decay products with a four-vector, for a total of $12$ degrees of freedom. Conservation of energy and momentum removes four degrees, the three masses $m_{\alpha}$ removes three, and finally the orientation of each product removes another three. Thus we are left wondering how best to choose the two remaining degrees of freedom. \\

A popular choice in nuclear and particle physics are the Dalitz coordinates
\begin{align} \label{theory: dalitz: xy}
	x = \sqrt{3}\frac{E_2 - E_3}{E_{tot}} && y = \frac{3E_1}{E_{tot}} - 1
\end{align}

devised by R. H. Dalitz back in 1953 \cite{Dalitz}. With these coordinates, each of the measured three-body final states are represented by a single point. Energy and momentum conservation restricts the points to fall within an equilateral triangle and an inscribed circle, respectively. It can be shown that within these areas, the phase space volume is constant \cite{Wuethrich}, such that any structure which may arise is due to properties of the reaction itself. An illustration of this type of plot can be seen on figure \ref{fig: theory: dalitz: dalitz}. \\

\begin{figure}[tb]

By ordering the energies such that $E_1 \geq E_2 \geq E_3$, all events must fall within the hatched area. }

Since we are dealing with three identical bosons, we are free to label them however we please. This introduces a six-fold symmetry to our Dalitz plots, which can be realized by defining one specific order, e.g. $E_1 \geq E_2 \geq E_3$. This ordering makes all events fall within the hatched area on figure \ref{fig: theory: dalitz: dalitz}. Although this single slice contains all the information of the decay, since it is easier to see structure on the full plot, we will mainly be using those. \\

Further studies on the effects of symmetries in Dalitz plots where performed by Zemach in 1964 \cite{Zemach}, where it was shown that some areas of the plot are suppressed, depending on the nature of the parent state. Although the study was for triple pion decay, it is just as applicable for our $\alpha$ decay. The suppressed regions are shown on figure \ref{fig: theory: dalitz: zemach}. Although no restrictions are imposed on our $0^+$ resonance, it will be useful for some of the other resonances we will study later.\\

\subsection{Angular correlations} \label{sec: theory: angular correlations}


\note{introduce the effect when you understand it} \\

The formalism used in this thesis follows the work of Biedenharn \cite{Biedenharn}, as explained in appendix D of the book \textit{Nuclear Physics of Stars} \cite{angular correlation appendix}. The correlation between the two decay products can be expressed as a series of Legendre polynomials in the angle between them
\begin{align} \label{theory: angular correlations: W}
	W(\beta) = \frac{1}{b_0} \sum_{n=0}^{N}b_n P_n(\cos\beta)
\end{align}

Note that in this notation, the angle $\beta$ is defined in relation to the direction of motion of the primary particle, so $\beta = \pi - \theta$. Remember that we assumed that each stage of the reaction has a well-defined parity. Thus the wave function describing the exit channel must have the same parity as the intermediate state. The intensity of the emitted radiation (square of the wave function) must then always have even parity, meaning it is unchanged by the parity transformation $\textbf{r} \rightarrow -\textbf{r}$, which we can express in terms of $\beta$ as $\beta \rightarrow \pi - \beta$, since the intensity does not depend on the azimuthal angle $\phi$ for unpolarized beams and randomly oriented target nuclei. This condition carries over to the Legendre polynomials, such that we require $W(\beta) = W(\pi - \beta)$, meaning they are all symmetric about $\beta = \pi/2$. This condition is only satisfied by all even polynomials, and so the odd ones must vanish. \\

The $b_n$ coefficients in (\ref{theory: angular correlations: W}) depend on the quantum properties of the interacting nuclei. Biedenharn \cite{Biedenharn} calculated the related coefficients $F_n$, given as
\begin{align*}
	F_n(LL'jJ) \equiv (-1)^{j-J-1}\sqrt{(2L+1)(2L'+1)(2J+1)}\langle L1L'-1|n0\rangle W(JJLL';nj)
\end{align*}
where $\langle L1L'-1|n0 \rangle$ and $W(JJLL';nj)$ denotes a Clebsh-Gordan and a Racah coefficient, respectively. The symmetry properties of these coefficients limit how many terms are needed in the sum, as $F_n(LLjJ) \neq 0$ only for ${0 < n \leq \min(2J, 2L)}$. \\

In a two-step decay process, the angular correlation can then be written in terms of $F_n$ as
\begin{align*}

\end{align*}

where $a_n(l)$ is given as
\begin{align*}
	a_n(l) = \frac{2l(l+1)}{2l(l+1) - n(n+1)}
\end{align*}

and $n$ runs from $0$ to $\min(2l_1, 2l_2, 2J)$. \subsection{Statistics} \label{section: theory: statistics}
Suppose we have a set of statistically independent quantities $\bm{x}$, all distributed with the same probability density function $f(x, \bm{\theta})$. Here $\bm{\theta}$ is some arbitrary set of parameters on which the distribution function depends, which we would like to estimate. One way of doing this is by constructing the likelihood function
\begin{align} \label{theory: statistics: likelihood}
	\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) = \prod_{n}^{i}f(x_i; \bm{\theta})
\end{align}
which, roughly speaking, describes the combined probability (or likelihood) of observing $\bm{x}$ given the density function $f(x, \bm{\theta})$ for some choice of parameters $\bm{\theta}$. The basic idea is then that by maximizing this probability, we obtain the best estimate of our parameters $\bm{\theta}$. Sometimes the number of events $n$ also depends on the parameters. If this is the case, the probability of observing exactly $n$ events should be included in the likelihood. Since we are dealing with Poisson-distributed data, (\ref{theory: statistics: likelihood}) is modified to 
\begin{align} \label{theory: statistics: extended likelihood}
	\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) = \frac{\mu^n}{n!}e^{-\mu}\prod_{n}^{i}f(x_i; \bm{\theta})
\end{align}
which is often called the extended likelihood. \\

When we apply this idea to our binned histogram data, the density function $f(x, \bm{\theta})$ is that of a multinomial distribution. Inserting this into (\ref{theory: statistics: extended likelihood}) and taking the natural logarithm for numerical stability, we get
\begin{align}
	\ln \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) = \sum^n_i  \ln \frac{\mu_i}{m_i} + m_i - \mu_i
\end{align}

Here $\mu$ is the expected number of events in each bin, while $m$ is the observed number. It is thus by maximizing this equation that we obtain the maximum likelihood estimate for our parameters $\bm{\theta}$. \\

To obtain a goodness-of-fit test statistic, one can instead minimize the likelihood ratio $-2\lambda = -2\ln f(\bm{m}; \bm{\theta})/f(\bm{m}; \bm{\mu})$, where the denominator is to be understood as the values one would find without any errors. \note{ask Hans}. As per Wilk's theorem, this quantity will asymptotically approach a $\chi^2$ distribution with $\mu - \theta$ degrees of freedom. Since $\bm{\mu}$ is generally unknown, we can replace it by $\bm{m}$ \cite{Kuhlwein}. \\

The final issue is how to deal with errors. For finite samples, the covariance matrix can result in an underestimate of the variances. A numerically equivalent approach to determine the $s$-standard deviation confidence interval is by finding the set of parameters $\bm{\theta}'$ where \note{cite https://pdg.lbl.gov/2013/reviews/rpp2013-rev-statistics.pdf}
\begin{align*}
	\ln \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}') = \ln \mathcal{L}_{max} - s^2/2
\end{align*}
This will in general result in asymmetrical errors on the parameters. \\

When we want to fit Monte Carlo simulations to our data, we introduce a statistical uncertainty to expected number of events $\mu_i$ since the values from the simulation are only an approximation. To account for this, the maximum likelihood statistic introduced here must be modified as seen in \cite{Kuhlwein}.

When we want to fit Monte Carlo simulations to our data, we introduce a statistical uncertainty to expected number of events $\mu_i$ since the values from the simulation are only an approximation. The goal of this section is to modify the maximum likelihood statistic to account for this. \\
